In this lesson, we look inside the edge radius macro, O sixteen eighteen.
The same macro makes a convex radius, which rounds the corner, and a concave one, which cuts a cove.

In the main program, the G sixty-five P sixteen eighteen line calls the macro. This example is a convex radius, R ten.
X: where the edge starts, minus two hundred. I: length, two hundred. Y: the edge face, minus twenty. W: the top.
C: the radius, ten. U: the type, zero convex and one concave. V: which side the cutter is on.
H: how many degrees each pass moves, one. T: a fifty millimeter cutter. A: clearance at the ends. R and F: clearance plane and feed.
Each letter becomes a variable: C becomes variable three, U twenty-one, V twenty-two, H eleven, and T twenty.

First, the checks: alarms one seventy-one to one seventy-six.
For example, H must be less than ninety degrees, otherwise alarm one seventy-three. Here H is one: it goes on.
Then variable one hundred stores the side. With V zero, it is one: the cutter is on the lower Y side, and the part is toward plus Y.

The cutter cuts the whole edge, end to end, and goes out past both ends.
Variable one oh one is the left end: X, minus half the cutter, twenty-five, minus the clearance, five. That is minus two hundred thirty.
One oh two is the right end: X plus I, plus twenty-five, plus five. That is thirty.

Now the main idea. The bottom corner of the cutter, on the part side, is what touches the curve.
The position on the curve is given by an angle, theta, measured from the radius center.
Variable one oh three is that angle. It starts at H, one degree.
Each pass adds H: one, two, three degrees, and so on, while it is less than ninety.

For the convex radius, the macro does two calculations with sine and cosine.
Variable one oh four is the distance from the face to the cutter corner: C minus C times sine of theta. At thirty degrees: ten minus five is five.
One oh six is the Z: W minus C, plus C times cosine of theta. At thirty degrees: minus ten plus eight point six six is minus one point three four.
At sixty degrees, the corner is closer to the face, one point three four, and deeper: Z minus five.

With the corner in place, we still need the cutter center. Variable one oh five is the center Y.
It is the face, minus twenty, plus one oh four, five, minus half the cutter, twenty-five. That is minus forty.
So the machine runs G one, Y minus forty, Z minus one point three four: it steps down to the new pass.

Then G one X one oh eight cuts the edge to the other end.
And the macro swaps the ends: one oh nine keeps one oh seven, one oh seven gets one oh eight, and one oh eight gets what was kept.
So one pass goes right and the next one comes back left. No moving in the air.

And the concave one? It is the same macro, with U one.
With U greater than zero, the IF at N ten jumps to N twenty.
For concave, the radius center is right on the edge. One oh four is C times cosine of theta, and Z is W minus C times sine of theta.
Example with C fifteen, at thirty degrees: the corner is twelve point nine nine from the face, at Z minus seven point five. The rest is the same.

At the end of each pass, variable one oh three adds H.
The IF asks: is it still less than ninety? If yes, back to N ten for the next pass.
With H one that is eighty-nine passes. After the last one, the cutter goes up to the clearance plane and M ninety-nine returns to the main program.
A smaller H makes a smoother curve, but takes longer. A larger H is faster, with bigger steps.

In short: the X ends, the angle that moves by H, the distance and Z with sine and cosine, the center Y and the back and forth.
On the first part, test with W fifty: the cutter does everything in the air.
